## Frameworks and recurring criteria

### Key Dance assessment frameworks found

The completed batches do not produce one single Dance assessment framework. They produce a layered evidence model.

Official school and conservatoire sources are strongest on **process architecture**. They consistently show preliminary screening by classwork or video, followed by richer live stages that may include technique classes, improvisation, solos, workshops, interviews, physical checks or directional response. Ballet-heavy institutions more often observe dancers through class structure and training potential, while contemporary-led institutions more often use workshop, improvisation and discussion structures.

RAD and ISTD provide the most stable **formal descriptor language**. RAD is the strongest formal source for ballet-inflected criteria such as posture, placement, coordination, control, line, timing, musical responsiveness, expression, interpretation and communication. ISTD contributes the most useful cross-style specificity outside ballet: contemporary adds breath, weight, flow, alignment, space use and creative response; modern theatre adds musicality, projection, style/theme, line and space; tap adds clarity of beating, rhythmic interpretation and precision of footwork.

Competition and adjudication frameworks provide **comparator vocabulary**, not direct design rules. They sharpen language on individuality, synchronisation, authenticity, showmanship, audibility and head-to-toe choreography, but they remain competition-shaped and often include logic that cannot be generalised.

Professional company and employer sources are strongest for **actual audition practice**. They show how taped material is used before live stages, what combinations of styles employers ask for, how quickly choreography may be learned before cuts, and how much remains callback-only or room-only. B4 materially improves this evidence base through Candoco, Equity and current cruise/employer calls.

Inclusive-dance and accessibility sources provide the strongest **anti-bias and non-penalisation framework**. They distinguish between changing process and changing standards, support open movement language and adaptation to body or pace, and reject capability assumptions, production-value bias and access-related penalisation.

### Dance self-tape and video assessability implications

The completed Dance evidence is unusually strong on one point: **video assessability is a condition of fair judgement, not a secondary presentation issue**. Central, USC and Candoco all support the idea that the tape must let the assessor actually see the moving body. That requires full-body or head-to-toe framing, a stable camera, enough distance to read full phrases, enough proximity to see body organisation, minimal editing, and music or audio that makes rhythmic judgement possible. London Studio Centre and Urdang strengthen this by showing that different styles require different kinds of submitted material, not a one-size-fits-all “dance reel”.

The strongest anti-bias boundary comes from Candoco and Equity: a dance self-tape does **not** need expensive production values. A smartphone or simple domestic setup can be acceptable if it clearly reveals the dancer’s movement, technique lineage, improvisational style, range and task response. The commercials self-tape code pushes the same principle further: the performance should be judged, not the equipment, props or transformed domestic environment.

The evidence also clarifies what video **cannot** reliably do. A finished tape can show execution, phrasing, style fit, some improvisation or solo quality, and sometimes partnering. It usually cannot reliably demonstrate response to live direction, real-time adaptability, pace of pickup, fatigue behaviour across a long day, interview readiness or some workshop/seminar discussion capacities. These should be kept distinct in later audit work.

A useful secondary distinction appears between **stage dance** and **screen dance**. U.DANCE explicitly separates stage and screen strands, which means filmed dance can be its own artistic form rather than a neutral capture of stage movement. Unless the brief is explicitly for screen dance, later Dance reporting should treat camera effects and editing primarily as assessability issues, not artistic value multipliers.

## Accessibility, fairness, exclusions and anti-generic language

### Accessibility, fairness and non-penalisation findings

The strongest cross-source finding is that **process adaptation and standards are separable**. RAD and ISTD policies establish the formal principle: reasonable adjustments may change process and remove barriers, but should not invalidate standards or confer unfair advantage. Candoco, Stopgap and Equity then translate this into dance-specific practice.

Across these sources, assessability and fairness imply the following:

| Domain | Evidence-supported synthesis |
|---|---|
| Assessability | The assessor needs a clear, fair view of movement, music relation and phrase structure before making technical or musical claims. Weak framing or audio should reduce confidence, not trigger invented criticism. |
| Process adaptation | Formats, pacing, advance information, access communication, task wording, room access, scheduling and material presentation can all be adapted. |
| Movement quality | Movement should still be assessed against the attained standard visible in the material, but the visible route to that attainment may be body-specific or access-shaped. |
| Access needs | Access needs, mobility aids, bespoke training pathways, CV gaps or convalescence must not be treated as negative signals where sources explicitly reject that logic. |
| Disability or injury inference | Sources support the opposite of speculative inference: do not assume medical history, capability ceilings or injury from appearance or adaptation. |
| Professional readiness | Professional readiness may include preparation, response to the brief and process participation, but not equipment privilege, body conformity or access-free biography. |

The B4 sources materially improve the synthesis here. Candoco explicitly normalises bespoke pathways, non-standard training histories and smartphone capture. Stopgap shows inclusive live structures with set material, improvisation and creative tasking. Equity provides the strongest operational fairness detail: accessible formats, advance information, detailed running order, fatigue-aware scheduling, open movement language, adaptable technique classes, adaptation to body and pace of learning, and a clear warning against capability assumptions.

### Final reusable Dance handoff pack for DANCE-AUDIT

#### Dance synthesis handoff

The Dance synthesis is now ready to move into audit. The strongest cross-source conclusion is that Dance cannot be treated as a single undifferentiated assessment domain. The completed B1–B4 corpus supports a layered model. Admissions and conservatoire sources define process structure, training-potential logic and what can be tested from preliminary video versus later live stages. Exam-board sources — especially RAD and ISTD — provide the most stable descriptor language for technique, musicality, performance quality and style. Professional company and employer sources show how those ideas are operationalised in real auditions, especially around reels, style asks, multi-combo learning, callbacks and live-only evidence. Accessibility, inclusive-dance and self-tape guidance define non-penalisation, assessability and anti-bias boundaries.

The strongest evidence-supported criteria across styles are not generic praise terms. They include placement/posture, coordination, control, clarity, line where relevant, use of space, rhythm/timing, musical responsiveness, style awareness, artistry/communication, and — in some contexts — improvisation or creative response. Those criteria must remain style-specific. Ballet, contemporary, jazz/modern theatre, tap, commercial and street/hip-hop all draw on overlapping domains, but not in the same way. Tap depends more heavily on audibility and rhythmic precision. Contemporary gives more weight to breath, weight, flow and creative tasking. Street and hip-hop require caution because the evidence base is improved but still partly competition- or employer-shaped.

The clearest non-regression theme is tape assessability. Full-body visibility, stable framing, audible music, simple setup and minimal editing are not cosmetic preferences; they are fairness conditions. Candoco and Equity further show that production value must not proxy talent, and that smartphone or domestic capture can still be valid if the movement is readable. Accessibility sources also make clear that process may adapt without access needs becoming negative evidence.

The main unresolved limits do not block audit. They concern later caution around universal commercial/street/hip-hop descriptors, tap outside exam boards, pickup speed as a formal descriptor, stamina overclaim, response-to-direction from finished tapes, and the current user-facing “vocal” proxy / dance-only labelling issue.

#### Tape-observable versus live-room-only summary

Tape can support credible claims about visible execution, phrase clarity, some musicality, style fit, whole-body coordination, solo quality and — where shown well — some improvisation or partnering. Tape is much weaker for pace of pickup, response to direction, fatigue over a long day, interview readiness and some workshop or callback capacities. These should become major audit boundaries.

#### Accessibility and fairness summary

Audit should preserve the principle that process can adapt without standards being discarded. Access needs, mobility differences, bespoke training pathways, CV gaps, convalescence, equipment constraints and domestic filming conditions must not be scored as deficits. Later audit should also separate assessability limitations from performer limitations.

#### Generic-feedback risk list

Later audit should target and replace unsupported language such as “good energy”, “nice movement”, “strong performance”, “good control”, “strong technique”, “good musicality”, “great presence”, “expressive”, “clear style”, “good rhythm”, “professional tape”, “confident performer”, “clean lines” and “good dynamics” unless each phrase is anchored to observable evidence.

#### Exclusion / anti-bias list

Later audit should explicitly block or tightly constrain: body type, appearance, height/physique as universal criteria, marketability, “bookability”, follower count, fame, social-media behaviour, disability as deficit, injury diagnosis, stamina claims from short clips, direction-response claims from a finished tape, expensive production value, studio polish, and singing/voice scoring in dance-only situations unless singing is actually present.

#### Audit priorities

The highest-priority audit topics are: the vocal-proxy risk, style-specific technique language, video assessability thresholds, audio/music assessability, live-only versus tape-observable boundaries, accessibility-safe assessment, professional-presentation boundaries, and removal of generic or bias-prone language.
