## Source-by-source evidence notes

**ACTING-S038 — ScreenSkills accessible audition standard**  
This standard explicitly treats accessible audition design as barrier-identification and barrier-removal work. It tells productions to identify barriers in the audition process and venue, confirm access requirements, adapt the process or location where needed, and respond to known requirements. Its scope is broader than step-free entry: it names environment, lighting, sound, transport, physical contact, personal space, timeframes and sensitivities, and it lists concrete resources such as braille printing, text-to-speech software, adaptive hardware, support workers, interpreters and support animals. The glossary is strongly anti-deficit: disability is framed through social barriers and attitudes rather than impairment, and the overview explicitly says accessible audition work should “encourage incidental casting”. There is no direct acting-performance descriptor layer here, and no direct tape criterion beyond access factors such as sound and lighting. citeturn7view0turn7view1turn7view5turn4view12turn4view14turn7view3

**ACTING-S039 — ScreenSkills access coordinator skills**  
This page adds an access-first production and casting layer rather than a performance-evaluation layer. It explicitly covers recruitment, casting, filming, editing and release, and says access coordinators should be embedded early so that solutions are built in from the outset. The page names deaf, disabled and/or neurodivergent talent directly, sets out the four pillars of access as physical, sensory, communication and cognitive, and requires confidentiality, access passports/riders, accessible materials, accessible schedule/location updates and sourcing of Easy Reads, interpreters, support workers and accessible travel. A particularly important non-deficit line is that the access coordinator’s work should allow talent to focus on their role rather than their own advocacy. This is highly relevant to fairness, but it is still not a direct acting-descriptor source. citeturn2view0turn8view0turn8view1turn5view0

**ACTING-S040 — Graeae access guidance**  
Graeae’s page is the strongest B4 source for social-model language and anti-deficit framing. It says access is central to the organisation’s work, explains that disabled and neurodivergent people are disabled by barriers rather than by impairment, rejects the medical-model framing that people need to be “fixed”, and places responsibility on organisations to make adaptations and adjustments. It also shows practical access culture: bespoke access solutions, accessible/alternative formats, access consultancy, access riders, BSL, audio description, captioning, audio flyers and Easy Read materials. The page is valuable because it shows access features as integral to creative practice rather than as add-ons, but it does not provide acting-performance criteria or audition-specific descriptors. citeturn2view2turn2view3turn12view1turn12view5

**ACTING-S041 — Equity advice for visually impaired actors**  
This is the most specific B4 source for how an audition or work process should adapt for visually impaired actors. It warns against treating visual impairment as monolithic, says preferred terms should be checked with the individual, and then gives concrete audition guidance: ask how the actor wants to access scripts, provide materials well in advance, avoid PDFs, use editable Word documents, use large clear type, consider line-feeding or recordings, offer an access worker in workshop auditions, use descriptive rather than demonstrative directions, and support orientation to buildings, rooms, crews and sets. It also recommends clear verbal instructions and notes trip hazards on set. Crucially, it explicitly encourages incidental casting of visually impaired and other disabled actors, without turning the role into a disability-only brief. The page is condition-specific, but it is highly valuable for anti-assumption and non-deficit handling. citeturn8view2turn6view0turn6view1turn5view5

**ACTING-S042 — East 15 Acting and Contemporary Theatre**  
This page is not an accessibility framework. Its B4 value lies in contemporary/creative/physical form coverage. It describes a course built around European physical theatre, character development, creativity, actor/writer-musician-choreographer identities, film, devising, writing, physical expression, clown, Shakespeare, parody, short film, physical comedy and site-specific classical work. It also names observation, self-assessment, reflection, research, improvisation, character studies and reacting to emotional upheaval through varied texts. These are useful signals for later synthesis because they widen the acting-form map beyond classical/contemporary speeches alone. However, the page is overtly methodology-shaped, naming Stanislavski, Commedia dell’Arte and Gaulier, and it is a course-description page rather than an audition or regulated assessment framework. citeturn13view2turn11view0turn3view0turn5view9

**ACTING-S043 — East 15 Acting and Fight Performance**  
This page provides the clearest B4 evidence for embodied acting and specialist physical performance. It frames the course around physical dramatic action, stage combat and performance, martial arts, gymnastics, weapons systems, movement, acrobatics, tumbling, dance, choreography, story and character action within a stage fight, fights for film, scenes for camera, devised work and audition technique. It also shows that the form can involve different presentational demands across venues and briefs. That is useful gap-fill evidence for embodied and fight-based acting contexts. But the same page makes its limits very clear: it is a specialist course, it assumes strenuous physical demands and a possible physical background, and it explicitly draws on Stanislavski plus inside-out and outside-in approaches. None of that can be universalised into a default acting standard. citeturn13view0turn2view8turn3view3turn5view10turn5view13

## Reusable handoff and completion

**ACTING-B4 handoff summary**

ACTING-B4 has now completed the accessibility, fairness and gap-fill layer that remained incomplete after B1 to B3. The strongest gain is that the Acting branch now has a usable non-deficit access spine. ScreenSkills’ accessible-audition standard and access-coordinator guidance both frame access through barrier removal rather than impairment failure. Together they make clear that accessible acting assessment is not just about step-free entry: it can involve environment, lighting, sound, timing, personal space, information and communication barriers, access passports/riders, accessible materials, interpreters, support workers and confidential handling of access information. Just as importantly, ScreenSkills explicitly frames the work so that disabled, deaf and neurodivergent talent can focus on the role rather than on self-advocacy, and the accessible-audition standard explicitly encourages incidental casting. citeturn7view0turn7view1turn8view1

Graeae and Equity then sharpen the language and practice. Graeae provides the clearest social-model and anti-medical-model wording in the Acting source base so far, explicitly placing responsibility on organisations to make adaptations and adjustments for equality of access, and showing access features such as BSL, audio description, captioning, Easy Read and alternative formats as integral rather than peripheral. Equity’s visually impaired actors guidance is the most operational B4 source: editable scripts instead of PDFs, advance text, line-feeding, access workers, descriptive directing, clear verbal instructions, orientation to rooms and sets, and incidental casting without turning impairment into the whole role. That is enough to stop later synthesis from treating visual impairment, adaptation or communication support as acting deficits. citeturn2view3turn12view1turn8view2

B4 also adds partial form-gap evidence. East 15’s Acting and Contemporary Theatre page expands the map for devising, physical expressionism, clown, parody, short film, character studies and collaborative making. East 15’s Acting and Fight Performance page expands embodied and fight-based acting through stage combat, choreography, physical storytelling, fights for film and scenes for camera. These gains matter, but they come with a clear caution: both pages are specialist, school-shaped course descriptions with explicit methodology fingerprints, not universal acting-assessment standards. citeturn13view2turn13view0turn11view0turn5view10

The branch can now move to ACTING-SYN, but with explicit limitations. Comedy remains thin. Richer methodology-neutral positive acting descriptors remain thinner than ideal. Neurodivergence-specific acting-assessment detail is broader than it is operational. Speech/voice under access conditions is better covered as communication access than as a positive acting-descriptor set. Those limitations should be preserved in synthesis rather than treated as solved. citeturn5view9turn8view1turn8view2
