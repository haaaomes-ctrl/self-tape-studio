## Source-by-source evidence notes

### VOICE-S043 evidence note

British Voice Association evidence supports a practical, non-diagnostic voice-care boundary. Its public leaflets advise hydration, warm-up, rest, humidification, avoiding shouting or whispering through symptoms, and seeking medical advice if the voice remains changed for more than two weeks. The online-voice leaflet additionally shows that technology, headphones, microphones, breaks and room setup affect vocal load rather than artistic merit. The ageing and children’s-voice pages add two important fairness points: age-related change varies widely by person, and during adolescence healthy voices can sometimes sound unstable or “unhealthy”, so age or maturity should not be overread from a recording. This row can later support safety guardrails and age/voice-maturity cautions. It cannot support diagnosis, hormone inference, mental-state inference or any new scoring category. The evidence is mixed: partly tape-observable at the level of cautious description, but largely safety-only and contextual. It is broadly recurring and not merely one-school taste. citeturn5view1turn5view2turn17view0turn22view0

### VOICE-S044 evidence note

The equivalent S044 source, resolved to BAPAM, gives the clearest performer-specific safety language in the batch. It defines vocal overload in terms of prolonged use, loud use, shouting, screaming or extremes of range, and describes signs such as hoarseness, discomfort, reduced range and fatigue-like strain. It also connects overload risk to room acoustics, monitoring, amplification, dehydration, infection and rehearsal structure. Crucially, BAPAM says voice changes or throat discomfort lasting more than two weeks should trigger professional assessment and that specialist examination is needed to decide treatment. This can later support hard no-diagnosis rules and cautious wording about strain-like sound. It cannot support pathology labelling, treatment advice, or importing rehearsal/workload recommendations as scoring rules. The evidence is strongly safety-only and process-only, though some sign language is cautiously observable. citeturn21view0turn21view1turn7view1

### VOICE-S045 evidence note

The NHS and ENT UK pages provide a clean clinical boundary: hoarseness, lost voice and sore throat may look similar from the outside, but persistent or recurring symptoms need medical review rather than lay diagnosis. They also reiterate that loud talking, whispering, smoke, dust and dehydration can worsen symptoms. This row can support the claim that a finished tape must not be used to infer laryngitis, pathology, respiratory condition or similar diagnoses. It cannot support singing-rubric language beyond safety caution, and it adds no audition-specific access guidance. The evidence is clinical-information-only. citeturn8view1turn8view2

### VOICE-S046 evidence note

Musicians’ Union evidence is especially valuable because it pairs performer-health information with equality rights. On health, the MU directs singers towards specialist clinic assessment and repeats that nobody can know the correct treatment without viewing the vocal folds. On disability and neurodivergence, the MU frames reasonable adjustments as proactive fairness measures, says equality is about fair outcomes rather than identical treatment, and describes neurodiversity as natural human variation rather than deficit. It also points to hearing-health support and adaptive-instrument funding/access routes. This row can later support guardrails around reasonable adjustments, disabled and neurodivergent musician handling, and no-diagnosis principles. It cannot justify using legal thresholds, union benefits or educational methods as score logic. The evidence is mainly access/process and safety context, not tape-derived merit evidence. citeturn9view0turn9view2turn25view0turn25view1turn23view0

### VOICE-S047 evidence note

RNID evidence is the strongest hearing-access source in the batch. It covers captions, sign interpretation, hearing loops, streamers, assistive listening devices, speech-to-text tools, video-conferencing accessibility features and the fact that regulated communication support is preferable in formal settings. It also shows that listening duration and volume matter for hearing safety, and that many accessibility tools carry real costs. This row can later support hearing-difference guardrails, captioning and tech-access fairness, and the principle that hearability and communication access are not proxies for talent. It cannot support vocal scoring, nor can it justify treating assistive tech or captions as professionalism deficits. The evidence is access-only and hearing-safety-only. citeturn11view1turn11view2turn26view0turn26view1

### VOICE-S048 evidence note

Drake Music provides some of the most useful inclusive-process evidence in the whole B4 batch. Its application-access page explicitly argues that telling applicants merely to email for adjustments creates extra “disability admin”, consumes time and energy, and can deter applicants because of fear of refusal. It therefore supports proactive access support and alternative formats rather than putting extra burden on disabled applicants. Its technology and training pages frame accessible instruments, disability equality and mixed-format participation as barrier removal, not remediation of personal deficit. This row can later support inclusive application and submission guardrails, especially around not penalising people for how they need to access a process. It cannot support any direct singing-score criteria. The evidence is access/process-only and broadly recurring in inclusion practice. citeturn12view1turn12view2turn12view3

### VOICE-S049 evidence note

The Johns Hopkins page is high-authority evidence that gender-diverse voice work should be identity-led rather than stereotype-led. It explains that some people want more neutral or nonbinary outcomes rather than stereotypically masculine or feminine ones, and that vocal parameters such as pitch, resonance, articulation and nonverbal cues are options around identity and preference. It also notes that long-term self-modulation can leave a voice strained, weak or raspy, which reinforces the need for caution and support rather than judgement. This row can later support gender-diverse voice guardrails and anti-stereotype handling. It cannot support gendered role-fit assumptions, therapy-pathway judgements or medicalised inferences from tape. The evidence is clinical/support-only. citeturn13view1

### VOICE-S050 evidence note

Accent Bias Britain gives the strongest evidence in the batch against accent hierarchy. It states that all speakers have an accent, that “standard” accents are prestigious because of power rather than intrinsic quality, and that asking candidates to adopt a prestige accent is unrealistic and unfair. Its intervention findings also show that awareness can reduce accent-based differences in competence ratings and that decisions should focus on objective criteria, knowledge and skills rather than accent. This row can later support anti-bias guardrails for diction and intelligibility handling. It cannot support positive scoring descriptors for speech difference, and it remains accent-focused rather than covering the full speech-difference universe. The evidence is hiring-context and policy-context rather than tape-derived performance evidence. citeturn14view0turn14view1

### VOICE-S051 evidence note

VOICE-S051 remained partial, but still added useful access-equity evidence. The Digital Lifeline evaluation shows that devices, data, assistive technology, digital skills and support networks materially affect whether disabled people can participate online at all. The Take It Away guide and MU coverage show parallel equipment and funding barriers for disabled musicians through adaptive instruments and accessible technology. This row can later support the principle that access to devices, connectivity, assistive tech and specialist equipment must never be treated as a proxy for talent or effort. It cannot support song-self-tape capture rules in the narrow sense, and it does not directly resolve accompaniment inequality. The evidence is access-only and broad rather than audition specific. citeturn15view1turn15view3turn23view0

### Provisional VOICE-B4 findings list

- **VOICE-B4-F01** — B4 sources support a firm no-diagnosis rule for vocal-health language from tape.
- **VOICE-B4-F02** — B4 accessibility sources frame disability, neurodivergence, hearing difference, communication support and gender-diverse voice as access-and-identity matters, not talent deficits.
- **VOICE-B4-F03** — B4 resource-equity evidence strengthens the B3 anti-polish spine by showing that devices, data, assistive technology and adaptive equipment materially affect access and assessability.

### Key vocal-health and safety themes

- Observable strain-like or fatigued sound must remain descriptive, not diagnostic.
- Persistent symptoms belong with GP / ENT / specialist voice-clinic pathways, not with score logic.
- Workload, room acoustics, monitoring and rehearsal conditions affect vocal load and should not be overclaimed from a finished tape.
- Age, adolescence and hormonal context create further caution against over-reading sound.

### Key accessibility, fairness and inclusive voice themes

- Reasonable adjustments are proactive fairness measures.
- Hearing-access tools, captions, STT and assistive listening are legitimate supports.
- Neurodiversity is variation, not deficit.
- Accent prestige is social, not evidence of greater competence.
- Gender-diverse voice handling should be identity-led and non-stereotyped.

### Resource-inequality and anti-polish summary

- Device, data and assistive-tech access are unequal.
- Adaptive equipment and funding routes matter for disabled musicians.
- Home environment and kit quality affect assessability, not talent.
- These findings strengthen rather than replace the B3 assessability-first logic.
